Beatmatching

Also beatmixing

Lining up two songs so their beats hit at the same time.

On the decks Matching tempo and timing by ear, with the pitch fader and small nudges. A sync button does it for you on gear that has one.

See also Nudge Pitch fader

In Deckie Deckie does not beatmatch; it tells you where to set the pitch, so beatmatching is quick.

Moves Heard from DJs

Acapella

Also a cappella, pella, vocal

A singing part with no beat under it, played on its own or laid over another song’s beat.

On the decks Spelled “acapella” in DJ pools and on forums. DJs lay one over an instrumental, or ride it into a stretch of the playing song where nobody sings.

See also Vocal‑free gap Vocal‑free bars Long blend

In Deckie The crate file records vocal‑free gaps of 8 bars or more, where an acapella could fit. Deckie makes no acapellas; pointing at the gap on screen is planned.

B

Moves Heard from DJs

Backspin

Also spinback, spin back, rewind

The record is spun backwards for a beat with a whoosh, and the next song lands on the hit.

On the decks Both words are used. Video tutorials say “spinback” and “backspin”; UK and trance DJs tend to say spinback, US DJs and turntablists backspin. Fun, DJs agree, but cheesy if overused.

See also Vinyl brake

In Deckie Planned “Backspin”, among the cut moves in setup.

Structure and time

Bar

Also measure

A group of beats, usually four, that repeats.

On the decks DJs count blends in bars: 8‑bar, 16‑bar, 32‑bar.

See also Beat Phrase

In Deckie Blend lengths are labelled in bars. Most songs are four beats to the bar, but Deckie records the real number for each song, since some are in three.

Moves Heard from DJs

Bass swap

Only one song carries the deep bass at a time, so at some point the bass jumps from the old song to the new one.

On the decks Kill the bass of the incoming song, then trade the two at the start of a phrase. DJs say “swap the basslines” or “switch the lows”, and the forum rule of thumb is never two basslines at once.

Also called switch lows (forums), bass switch (forums), kill the bass (all, the incoming side), swap the basslines (house forums), EQ swap, low swap, hard swap (slang)

See also Blend Phrase

In Deckie Every blend Deckie suggests includes a bass swap; the label names the length of the blend, not the swap.

Beat

Also kick, count

The steady pulse you tap your foot to.

On the decks One count. Four make a bar in most dance music.

See also Bar Beat grid

In Deckie The analysis lists every beat and fits a steady grid to them.

Sound and analysis

Beat grid

Also beatgrid, grid

A ruler of evenly spaced beats laid over the song.

On the decks “Shaky beat grid” is a common DJ complaint: sync and quantize rely on the grid.

See also Beat Transition track

In Deckie Deckie fits one steady grid to the beats it hears and scores how well it fits. Bars and mix windows are measured against it; a poor fit makes Deckie prefer cuts and echo outs over blends.

Moves Heard from DJs

Blend

The two songs overlap for a while, and the low end moves from one to the other halfway through.

On the decks “Blend” is the everyday verb: DJs “blend tunes” with filters and EQ, around 16 bars in house. In hip‑hop mixtapes a blend is an acapella laid over another record’s beat.

Also called mix (all), beatmix, 16‑bar bass swap, EQ swap

See also Long blend Short blend Bass swap

In Deckie Shown as “Blend · 16 bars”, the middle length. The bar count is there so a hip‑hop DJ, for whom a blend means something else, reads it the house way.

Tempo and pitch

BPM

Also beats per minute

How many beats a song has in a minute; a number for how fast it feels.

On the decks The tempo as a number. DJs talk about gaps in BPM when they are small: within 3 BPM is easy.

See also Tempo Pitch fader

In Deckie Shown for the playing song at the deck’s current pitch, not at the song’s original speed, and on each pick beside the pitch to set.

On the decks The stripped‑back section before a drop.

In Deckie Deckie’s analysis finds the sections of a song and marks quiet ones in the middle as breaks.

Build

Also build‑up, riser

The part where the music climbs and tension grows.

On the decks The climb back after a breakdown, often with risers.

See also Breakdown Drop

Key and harmony

Camelot wheel

A clock face of keys numbered 1 to 12, so you can see at a glance which songs fit.

On the decks Codes 1A to 12B from Mixed In Key (A for minor, B for major); a step of +2 is what it calls an “energy boost”. Traktor’s Open Key is the same wheel, numbered differently.

Also called Camelot, Open Key, key code, 8A

See also Neighbour Relative key

In Deckie Deckie shows keys as Camelot codes and judges two songs by how far apart they sit on the wheel.

Moves Heard from DJs

Chop

Flicking between the two songs a bar or two at a time before letting the new one take over.

On the decks DJs “cut and chop” with the crossfader, switching back and forth between two songs. Not “chopped and screwed”, a slowed‑down style from Houston.

Also called cutting back and forth (forums), chop mix, fader cuts, switching back and forth (forums)

See also Hard cut Confidence

In Deckie Planned “Chop · back and forth” among the move tiles. Deckie cannot follow chops yet: it waits until one song clearly takes over before it changes the screen.

Confidence

Also sure, likely, unsure

How sure Deckie is that it has the right song.

See also Recognition

In Deckie The status line says sure, likely or unsure, from how many recent matches agree and how well their timing lines up. A new song replaces the old one on screen only once it clearly wins: a few seconds after a cut, about 10 seconds into a blend.

Crate

A group of songs a DJ pulls together, for a gig or for life. It can be the whole library.

Moves Heard from DJs

Cut on the one

The new song starts exactly on the first beat of a musical section, so the switch lands where it feels natural.

On the decks “Drop it on the one”, DJs say: start the new song on the first beat of a phrase. “Drop on the four” is when its singing starts a beat early.

Also called drop it on the one (hip‑hop, open format), on the one, dropmix, drop mixing (DJ schools)

See also Hard cut Downbeat Drop

In Deckie Planned As a kind of hard cut. Deckie uses “drop” only in this phrase, because alone it also means the big moment in a dance track.

Downbeat

Also the one, one

The first and strongest beat of each group.

On the decks Beat one of a bar. “On the one” means the first beat of a phrase, not just any bar.

See also Cut on the one Phrase

The big moment when everything comes back in.

On the decks Where everything lands. Also a verb, as in “drop it on the one”, and a radio jingle.

See also Build Cut on the one

Moves Deckie’s own phrase

Echo in

Also delay in, echoed entry, echo entry

The next song arrives through an echo: its first hits ring in over the playing song before it lands clean on the beat.

On the decks The mirror of echo out: send the next song’s first hits through an echo timed to the beat, then turn the echo off as it lands on the one. Tutorials teach the pieces but give it no name; “echo in” is Deckie’s.

See also Echo out Tease Scratch in

In Deckie Planned “Echo in”, a move tile paired with Echo out, suggested when the playing song has a steady beat and the next one starts sparse.

Moves Heard from DJs

Echo out

The old song stops on a beat and its echo keeps ringing while the new song starts.

On the decks Said as “echo out” or “echoed out” on forums and in video titles; “echo freeze” holds the tail. Stop the old song on the one and let its echo ring under the new one.

Also called echoed out (verb, forums), echo freeze (Traktor, rekordbox users), delay out, freeze (Traktor), wash‑out (tutorials), FX out

See also Reverb wash Loop roll Picks Echo in

In Deckie Shown as “Echo out”, when the singing would clash or the song ends without a steady beat.

Structure and time Deckie’s own phrase

Exposed vocal

Also dry vocal, vocal breakdown, the vocal on its own

A stretch where the voice carries on while the drums and bass drop away.

On the decks Producers say “the vocal is exposed” or “dry vocal”; DJs call the same bars a breakdown with vocals. Loud and clear, so it clashes with any other voice and sits well over a beat‑only intro.

See also Vocal‑free bars Vocal‑free gap Intro

In Deckie The crate file marks bars where the voice is left on its own, estimated from the low end and how busy the drums are, and labelled as an estimate.

Moves Heard from DJs

Fade

One song gets quieter while the next gets louder. The first thing anyone learns.

On the decks DJs say “fade out”, “crossfade” or “feather out”; mobile and wedding DJs ask for a “radio‑style fade”. No beatmatching needed.

Also called crossfade (all), fade out (all), feather out (forums), segue (radio, mobile), radio‑style fade (wedding DJs), fade mix

See also Blend Song chain

In Deckie Planned a move tile in setup called “Fade”, the name DJs are taught, rather than “fade mix”, which some DJs use for a blend.

Structure and time

Fade‑out

Also fade ending, cold ending

A song ending by slowly getting quieter rather than stopping.

On the decks The radio ending. Useless for a blend, because there is nothing steady to blend over.

See also Mix‑out window Outro

In Deckie Detected by the analysis. With a fade, the mix‑out window ends before the fade begins.

Tempo and pitch Deckie’s own phrase

Felt tempo

The speed you would clap along to, which can be half or double what a machine counts.

On the decks DJs say half time and double time: a slow song tagged 70 BPM may be played at 140 by feel. “Felt tempo” is Deckie’s name for the idea.

See also Half time How you mix

In Deckie When Deckie’s own reading is exactly half or double the tempo written in the file, it takes the file’s. Learning how each DJ hears tempo is planned.

Moves Heard from DJs

Filter sweep

The playing song gets thinner and thinner, as if heard through a wall, while the next one rises underneath.

On the decks DJs say “filter sweep” or “sweep the filter to high pass”: cut the lows until only the thin top is left. “Filter fade” is the tutorial name.

Also called filter fade (tutorials), sweep the filter (forums), lo‑cut (Pioneer DJs), HPF out, sweep out, filter mix

See also Blend Echo out

In Deckie Shown as “Filter sweep”, the word DJs say. Deckie avoids “filter out”, which a DJ trying an early version did not understand.

Recognition

Fingerprint

Also audio fingerprint, signature

A compact sound print of a song that a device can match what it hears against, without holding the song.

On the decks How song‑finder apps recognise music. A fingerprint cannot be turned back into audio.

See also Crate file

In Deckie Deckie Desk makes one from each of your songs, a few tens of kilobytes each, and they travel in the crate file. On the iPad, Apple’s ShazamKit matches against those alone, offline: Deckie carries no catalogue of other music. Today each matches within about 4 percent of the song’s own speed; covering the whole pitch fader is planned.

H

Moves Heard from DJs

Half time (and double time)

Two songs can match even when one is twice as fast as the other, because every second beat lines up.

On the decks Drum and bass and trap DJs say “halftime”; DJs mixing hip‑hop into dance music talk about “doubling” the slow song. 87 BPM against 174, 95 against 190.

Also called halftime (drum and bass, trap), double time, doubling (forums), 2x

See also Felt tempo

In Deckie A pick says “half time” or “double time” when one tempo is half or double the other. Such pairs rank a little lower than straight ones.

Moves Heard from DJs

Hard cut

One song stops and the next starts at once, with no overlap.

On the decks DJs just say “cut”: “quick cuts”, or “scratch and cut to the next track”. “Hard cut” is the plain‑English name.

Also called cut (all), quick cut (hip‑hop, open format), drop (US hip‑hop: drop the next record), slam, stop/start (tutorials)

See also Cut on the one Slam Vinyl brake Confidence

In Deckie Shown as “Hard cut”, never “hard break”. It is the fallback when the tempos or the beats cannot be matched.

Structure and time Heard from DJs

Hook

Also the chorus, the bit everyone knows, drop

The most recognisable few bars of a song, the part the crowd sings back.

On the decks Everyday DJ and producer word; in EDM the drop plays the same role. Teasing the hook of the next song is a classic move.

See also Drop Tease Phrase

In Deckie Deckie’s analysis proposes up to three likely hooks per song, from repetition, loudness and singing; confirming one with a tap is planned. It cannot know which line people know by heart: that is not in the audio.

How you mix

A page that shows what Deckie has learned about how you mix, with undo.

See also Song chain

In Deckie Planned about a dozen habits learned on the device, such as how far you push the pitch, how strict you are about key and how long you blend, and settings in DJ words, such as blends or cuts first, and whether two vocals may overlap. In key

Intro

Also DJ intro, intro edit

The opening of a song, before the main part.

On the decks A DJ intro is bars of beat only at the start, made for mixing in. Pop originals often have none.

K

Key and harmony

Key

Also tonal centre, key code

The home note a song is built around, which makes some songs sound good together and others sour.

On the decks The home note, plus major or minor. DJs say “key” for the music and “pitch” for the fader.

See also Camelot wheel In key Key clash Picks

In Deckie Each song gets a key, shown as a Camelot code, with any key change marked. No key reader is right every time, most often mixing up major and minor, so Deckie marks a key it is unsure of. A clash is a warning, not a score to chase.

Key and harmony

Key clash

Also clash, keys clash

Two songs in keys that sound sour together.

On the decks The DJ word for keys that do not fit. On purpose it can add tension; by accident it sounds forced.

See also In key Key shift

In Deckie A pick’s line says “key clash”. Planned: a key setting with three choices, Off, Flag clashes and In key first; In key first also stops the longest blends on a clash.

Tempo and pitch

Key lock (or master tempo)

A switch that lets you speed a song up without making the singer sound higher.

On the decks Keeps the key while the tempo changes. Pioneer calls it MASTER TEMPO; Serato and Traktor say Keylock. With it off, a faster record also sounds higher, the old vinyl way.

Also called master tempo, keylock, pitch lock, MT, time stretch

In Deckie Deckie reads the pitch either way: with key lock off, from how much higher or lower the song sounds; with it on, from how fast the song runs ahead of the clock. Today a key‑locked change past about 4 or 5 percent may not be recognised. A setup question about key lock is planned.

Moves Tutorial word

Key shift

Also Pitch ‘n Time, tonal shift, pitch shift

Moving a song up or down one note so it sits in tune with the other.

On the decks A word from the gear: KEY SHIFT on Pioneer’s CDJ‑3000, Pitch ‘n Time in Serato. DJs rarely change a song’s key, and when they do it is by one semitone.

See also Key Key clash Semitone

In Deckie Shown as “Key shift +1” (or −1, ±2), when a small shift turns a clash into a match.

Moves Heard from DJs

Long blend

Both songs play together for a long stretch, a minute or more, so the change is hard to hear.

On the decks Forum DJs say “long blends” or “long mixes”, and one puts the aim as “long and smooth, perfectly in phrase”. 16 to 32 bars is the house and techno default.

Also called long mix (house, techno, drum and bass), long and smooth (forums), seamless blend (tutorials), progressive mix, 32‑bar

The last beat repeats faster and faster, like a drum roll, and the next song lands on the hit.

On the decks Said as is, from the pad names in Serato and on Pioneer gear; Traktor users say Beatmasher. Not Serato’s old “Roll Out”, which is a brake‑style effect.

Also called roll (Pioneer), slip roll (Pioneer DJM), Beatmasher (Traktor users), beat roll, stutter

Mix‑in window

Also IN, intro window

The stretch at the start of a song that is best for bringing it in.

On the decks Where you would start the next song: a clean intro, ideally without singing.

Structure and time

Mix‑out window

Also SYNC, outro window

The stretch near the end of a song that is best for handing over.

On the decks Where you would start bringing the next song in: a clean outro, before the record ends or fades.

See also Sync area Mix‑in window Fade‑out

In Deckie Marked SYNC on the wave (the place, not the sync button), sized like the mix‑in window and ending before any fade‑out.

Key and harmony

Modulation (a key change)

Also key change, truck driver’s gear change

When a song changes key partway through, often a lift for the last chorus.

On the decks A key change inside the track; in pop, often a step up for the last chorus.

See also Song structure Confidence

In Deckie Deckie reads the key through the song and starts a new one only when the music moves and stays there. It also reads the key at each end of the song, where the mixing happens: a song that steps up for its last chorus ends in another key.
